Algebraic geometry

What shapes do the solutions of polynomial equations trace out, and how do two such shapes meet?

Algebraic geometry studies the sets of solutions of systems of polynomial equations, such as lines, conics, cubic curves and surfaces, and their higher-dimensional analogs, called varieties. It asks what shapes these sets have, how they intersect, how they vary in families, and when two of them are essentially the same.

The subject translates geometry into commutative algebra and back: a variety corresponds to a ring of functions on it. Schemes, sheaves and cohomology extend this dictionary so that it works over any field or ring, including the integers, which joins the subject to number theory. Over the complex numbers it meets complex analysis, topology and differential geometry, and it has close ties to theoretical physics.

Central objects

Affine variety
The set of common zeros of a collection of polynomials in several variables.
Projective space
Ordinary space completed by points at infinity, so that parallel lines meet.
Scheme
A space built by gluing spectra of commutative rings; the general object of the subject.
Morphism
A map between varieties given locally by polynomials or quotients of polynomials.
Sheaf
A rule assigning data, such as functions, to open sets in a way compatible with restriction and gluing.
Divisor
A formal integer combination of subvarieties of codimension one, such as zeros and poles of a function.
Moduli space
A variety or scheme whose points classify geometric objects of a given kind.

Landmark theorems

  1. Bézout's theorem

    Two projective plane curves of degrees ddd and eee over an algebraically closed field, with no common component, meet in exactly dedede points when points are counted with intersection multiplicity.

  2. Cayley-Salmon theorem1849

    Every smooth cubic surface in projective 3-space over an algebraically closed field contains exactly 27 lines.

  3. Riemann-Roch theorem for curves1865

    Let DDD be a divisor on a smooth projective curve of genus ggg over an algebraically closed field, and KKK a canonical divisor. Then ℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=deg⁡D+1−g\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-gℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=degD+1−g, where ℓ(D)\ell(D)ℓ(D) is the dimension of the space of rational functions fff with div⁡(f)+D≥0\operatorname{div}(f)+D\ge 0div(f)+D≥0, together with zero.

  4. Hironaka's resolution of singularities1964

    For every variety XXX over a field of characteristic 0 there are a smooth variety YYY and a proper birational morphism Y→XY\to XY→X.

  5. Weil conjectures1974

    Let XXX be a smooth projective variety over a field with qqq elements. Its zeta function, which counts the points of XXX over all finite extensions, is a rational function of q−sq^{-s}q−s, satisfies a functional equation, and has all zeros and poles of absolute value q−i/2q^{-i/2}q−i/2 for integers iii with 0≤i≤2dim⁡X0\le i\le 2\dim X0≤i≤2dimX. The last statement was proved in 1974.

  6. Faltings's theorem1983

    A smooth projective curve of genus at least 2 defined over a number field has only finitely many points with coordinates in that field.

Open problems and frontiers

  • Hodge conjecture

    On a smooth complex projective variety, every rational cohomology class of type (p,p)(p,p)(p,p) is a rational combination of classes of algebraic subvarieties.

  • Resolution of singularities in characteristic p

    Whether every variety over a field of positive characteristic has a resolution of singularities; open in dimension 4 and above.

  • Jacobian conjecture

    A polynomial map from Cn\mathbb{C}^nCn to itself whose Jacobian determinant is a nonzero constant has a polynomial inverse; open for every n≥2n\ge 2n≥2.
