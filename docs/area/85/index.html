85 Astronomy and astrophysics

Astronomy, astrophysics

How can we weigh a star or a galaxy that we can never visit?

This area contains the mathematics used to describe the universe beyond the Earth: the orbits of planets, moons and spacecraft, the structure and evolution of stars, the dynamics of galaxies and clusters, and the expansion of the universe as a whole. It also covers the passage of light through stellar matter and the methods used to turn observations into measurements.

The models combine gravity with fluid dynamics, thermodynamics, nuclear and atomic physics and radiation. They lead to ordinary differential equations for orbits and stellar interiors, partial differential equations for gas and radiation, and large particle simulations for galaxies. Because experiments are impossible, statistical inference from observations carries much weight. General relativity is required for compact objects and for cosmology.

Weighing the Sun

The Earth has a=1.496×1011a = 1.496\times10^{11}a=1.496×1011 m and T=3.156×107T = 3.156\times10^7T=3.156×107 s, and G=6.674×10−11G = 6.674\times10^{-11}G=6.674×10−11 in SI units. Central objects

Orbit
The path of a body moving under the gravitational pull of others.
Gravitational potential
A function whose gradient gives the gravitational force per unit mass.
Hydrostatic equilibrium
The balance in a star between the inward pull of gravity and the outward pressure gradient.
Radiative transfer
The description of how light is emitted, absorbed and scattered on its way through matter.
Redshift
The stretching of the wavelength of light from a receding or distant source.
N-body system
A collection of masses, such as a star cluster, each moving under the gravity of all the others.
Cosmological model
A solution of the equations of gravity describing the universe on the largest scales.
