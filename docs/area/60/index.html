60 Probability theory and stochastic processes

Probability theory

Why do averages of many random outcomes settle down, and how large are the fluctuations that remain?

Probability theory is the mathematics of chance. It assigns numbers between 0 and 1 to events and works out the consequences, from coin tossing to the motion of a pollen grain in water. Stochastic processes, which are random quantities that evolve in time, form a large part of the area. Examples are random walks, Markov chains and Brownian motion.

Since the 1930s the subject has been built on measure theory, with probabilities treated as measures and random variables as measurable functions. A recurring theme is that large random systems show regular behavior: averages converge and fluctuations follow universal laws. Probability supplies the language for statistics, statistical physics and mathematical finance, and probabilistic arguments are used in analysis, combinatorics and number theory.

Central objects

Probability space
A set of outcomes, a collection of events and a measure assigning each event a probability.
Random variable
A measurable function that assigns a number to each outcome.
Expectation
The probability-weighted average of a random variable.
Independence
Events or variables are independent when the probability of joint occurrences is the product of individual probabilities.
Markov chain
A random process whose next step depends only on its current state.
Brownian motion
A continuous random path with independent, normally distributed increments.
Martingale
A process whose expected future value, given the past, equals its present value.
