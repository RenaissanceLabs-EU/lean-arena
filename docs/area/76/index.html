76 Fluid mechanics

Fluid mechanics

Why does smoke rise in a smooth column and then suddenly break up into swirls?

Fluid mechanics describes the motion of liquids and gases: water in pipes and oceans, air around wings, blood in arteries, plasma in stars. The fluid is modeled as a continuum with a velocity, pressure and density at every point. The area covers viscous and inviscid flow, compressible gases and shock waves, waves, stability, turbulence, flow in porous media and electrically conducting fluids.

The basic equations are nonlinear partial differential equations expressing conservation of mass, momentum and energy. Exact solutions are rare, so the area relies on scaling arguments, asymptotic approximations such as boundary layer theory, stability analysis and numerical simulation. Whether the equations always have smooth solutions is itself a major open question in the theory of partial differential equations.

Smooth or turbulent?

The Reynolds number Re=UL/νRe = UL/\nuRe=UL/ν compares inertia with viscosity, using a speed UUU, a length LLL and the kinematic viscosity ν\nuν. Water, with ν≈10−6 m2/s\nu \approx 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}ν≈10−6 m2/s, flowing at 1 m/s through a pipe 2 cm wide has Re=20000Re = 20000Re=20000, and the flow is turbulent. A syrup ten thousand times more viscous gives Re=2Re = 2Re=2, and the flow is smooth. Pipe flow typically turns turbulent at ReReRe of a few thousand.

Central objects

Velocity field
The velocity of the fluid at each point of space and each time.
Pressure
The force per unit area that the fluid exerts equally in all directions.
Viscosity
A measure of internal friction, the resistance of a fluid to shearing.
Vorticity
The curl of the velocity field, measuring local rotation.
Reynolds number
A dimensionless ratio of inertial to viscous effects.
Boundary layer
A thin region near a solid wall where viscosity matters even when it is negligible elsewhere.
Shock wave
A thin surface across which pressure, density and velocity of a gas change abruptly.

Open problems and frontiers

  • Navier-Stokes regularity

    Do smooth, finite-energy initial data in three dimensions always give a solution of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations that stays smooth for all time?

  • Blow-up for the Euler equations

    Can a smooth, finite-energy solution of the three-dimensional incompressible Euler equations in the whole space become singular in finite time?

  • Theory of turbulence

    Derive the observed statistical laws of turbulent flow, such as Kolmogorov's spectrum and its corrections, from the Navier-Stokes equations.
