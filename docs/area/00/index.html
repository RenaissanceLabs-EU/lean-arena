General topics

What is a proof, and how does mathematics organize, explain and share what it knows?

This class collects writing about mathematics as a whole instead of one branch. It covers general surveys, books for nonspecialists, problem books, recreational mathematics, popular exposition, dictionaries and formula collections, the philosophy and methodology of mathematics, and conference proceedings. It also covers general questions of mathematical modeling, such as dimensional analysis, and the links between mathematics and music, art, architecture and literature.

There is no single method here. What unites the material is a view from above: how proofs work, how definitions are chosen, how a model is matched to the world, and how results are explained to wider audiences. Many ideas that every mathematician uses, such as induction, proof by contradiction and counting arguments, are met first in books that belong to this class.

Units decide the answer

How does the period TTT of a pendulum depend on its length ℓ\ellℓ, its mass mmm and gravity ggg? Length is in meters, ggg in meters per second squared, mass in kilograms. The only combination with units of seconds is ℓ/g\sqrt{\ell/g}ℓ/g​. So T=Cℓ/gT=C\sqrt{\ell/g}T=Cℓ/g​, where the pure number CCC may depend on the swing angle. Mass cannot matter, and at the same angle a pendulum four times as long takes twice as long.

Central objects

Proof
A finite chain of logical steps that establishes a statement from accepted assumptions.
Axiom
A statement taken as a starting point without proof.
Definition
A precise agreement on what a term means, on which all later reasoning depends.
Conjecture
A statement believed to be true for which no proof is known.
Mathematical model
A mathematical description of a real system, used to explain or predict its behavior.
Dimensional analysis
Reasoning about physical formulas from the units of the quantities involved.
Exposition
Writing that explains known mathematics to students, other specialists or the public.

Landmark theorems

  1. Principle of mathematical induction

    Let P(n)P(n)P(n) be a statement about natural numbers. If P(0)P(0)P(0) holds and P(n)P(n)P(n) implies P(n+1)P(n+1)P(n+1) for every nnn, then P(n)P(n)P(n) holds for every natural number nnn.

  2. Pigeonhole principle

    If n+1n+1n+1 objects are placed in nnn boxes, then some box contains at least two objects.

  3. Irrationality of the square root of 2

    There are no positive integers ppp and qqq with p2=2q2p^2=2q^2p2=2q2. Hence 2\sqrt22​ is not a ratio of whole numbers.

  4. Euler's theorem on the Königsberg bridges1736

    A connected finite graph, possibly with several edges between two vertices, has a closed walk that uses every edge exactly once if and only if every vertex has even degree. Euler showed that the condition is necessary, which settles the Königsberg bridge puzzle; sufficiency was proved later.

  5. Buckingham pi theorem1914

    Suppose a physical law relates nnn quantities, and the matrix of exponents expressing their units in terms of base units has rank kkk. If the law holds in every system of units, it can be rewritten as a relation among n−kn-kn−k dimensionless products of the quantities.
