53 Differential geometry

Differential geometry

How can an ant living on a surface tell, without ever leaving it, whether the surface is curved?

Differential geometry applies calculus to curved spaces: curves, surfaces and their higher-dimensional versions, smooth manifolds. Its basic question is how to define and measure curvature, and what curvature implies about shape. A Riemannian metric gives lengths and angles on a manifold, and from it come geodesics, volume and curvature. The area also studies other structures on manifolds, such as symplectic, complex, contact and Lorentzian ones.

Methods combine tensor calculus, connections and differential forms with differential equations and topology. A recurring theme is the passage from local to global: bounds on curvature at each point constrain the topology and size of the whole space. Geometric flows deform a shape in order to improve it. General relativity models gravity as curvature of spacetime, and gauge theories in physics use connections on bundles, so exchange with physics is constant.

A triangle with three right angles

On a sphere of radius 1, draw a triangle with one corner at the north pole and two on the equator, a quarter turn apart. Its sides are arcs of great circles and all three angles are right angles, so they sum to 270∘270^\circ270∘, not 180∘180^\circ180∘. The excess, π/2\pi/2π/2 in radians, equals the area of the triangle, which is one eighth of 4π4\pi4π. The excess measures the curvature enclosed: here the Gaussian curvature is 1.

What to notice
  • The sphere is curved the same way everywhere (K > 0), the cylinder has K = 0 and can be unrolled flat, the saddle has K < 0 everywhere. The torus has all three kinds of point.
  • On the sphere, the angles of a triangle always add up to more than 180 degrees.
  • The excess over 180 degrees, in radians, equals the triangle's area on the unit sphere. The area is computed separately, by a different formula.

Central objects

Smooth manifold
A space that near every point looks like Euclidean space, with smooth changes of coordinates.
Riemannian metric
A smoothly varying inner product on the tangent spaces, giving lengths and angles.
Geodesic
A curve with zero acceleration; short pieces of it are shortest paths.
Connection
A rule for differentiating vector fields, which defines parallel transport along curves.
Curvature tensor
The tensor recording how parallel transport around small loops fails to return vectors unchanged.
Gaussian curvature
The product of the two principal curvatures of a surface at a point.
Symplectic form
A closed nondegenerate differential 2-form, the geometric structure of classical mechanics.

Landmark theorems

  1. Theorema Egregium1827

    The Gaussian curvature of a surface in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 is determined by the first fundamental form alone. Hence it is preserved by local isometries.

  2. Gauss-Bonnet theorem

    For a compact oriented Riemannian surface MMM without boundary, with Gaussian curvature KKK and Euler characteristic χ(M)\chi(M)χ(M), ∫MK dA=2πχ(M)\int_MK\,dA=2\pi\chi(M)∫M​KdA=2πχ(M).

  3. Fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry

    On every Riemannian manifold there is exactly one connection that is torsion-free and compatible with the metric, the Levi-Civita connection.

  4. Hopf-Rinow theorem1931

    For a connected Riemannian manifold the following are equivalent: it is complete as a metric space; every geodesic extends to all values of its parameter; closed bounded subsets are compact. When they hold, any two points are joined by a geodesic of least length.

  5. Bonnet-Myers theorem1941

    Let MMM be a complete Riemannian manifold of dimension nnn whose Ricci curvature satisfies Ric≥(n−1)k\mathrm{Ric}\ge(n-1)kRic≥(n−1)k for a constant k>0k>0k>0. Then MMM is compact with diameter at most π/k\pi/\sqrt{k}π/k​, and its fundamental group is finite.

  6. Nash embedding theorem1956

    Every smooth Riemannian manifold admits a smooth isometric embedding into a Euclidean space of sufficiently high dimension.
