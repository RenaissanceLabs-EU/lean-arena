82 Statistical mechanics, structure of matter

Statistical mechanics

How do countless jostling molecules add up to something as simple as temperature and pressure?

Statistical mechanics explains the behavior of matter in bulk from the laws governing its microscopic parts. Because a glass of water contains far too many molecules to follow one by one, the theory works with probability distributions over microscopic states. It derives thermodynamic quantities, explains phase transitions such as boiling and magnetization, and describes how systems approach equilibrium.

In equilibrium the central object is the partition function, a sum over all states weighted by their energies, and the main questions concern its behavior as the system grows. Lattice models, such as the Ising model of a magnet, serve as test cases. The area uses probability, combinatorics, analysis and operator algebras, and its ideas, notably the renormalization group and Monte Carlo sampling, are now used well outside physics.

Central objects

Microstate
A complete specification of the state of every particle in the system.
Partition function
The sum Z=∑e−E/kTZ = \sum e^{-E/kT}Z=∑e−E/kT over all microstates, from which thermodynamic quantities are computed.
Gibbs measure
The probability distribution on microstates describing a system in thermal equilibrium.
Free energy
The quantity −kTlog⁡Z-kT\log Z−kTlogZ, minimized at equilibrium for fixed temperature.
Phase transition
An abrupt change in bulk behavior, such as melting, as a parameter like temperature varies.
Ising model
A lattice of spins taking values plus or minus one, with neighboring spins favoring agreement.
Critical exponent
A number describing how a quantity diverges or vanishes near a continuous phase transition.

    The nearest-neighbor ferromagnetic Ising model on the square lattice with zero external field has spontaneous magnetization at all sufficiently low temperatures.

  6. Mermin-Wagner theorem1966

    In one and two dimensions, the quantum Heisenberg model with short-range interactions has no spontaneous magnetization at any positive temperature. More generally, a continuous symmetry cannot be spontaneously broken in such systems.
