Group theory

What do the symmetries of a square, a shuffled deck of cards and a Rubik's cube have in common?

Group theory is the mathematics of symmetry. A group is a set with one operation that is associative, has an identity element and has inverses. The rotations and reflections of a square, the permutations of a list, and the integers under addition are examples. The area also covers looser structures such as semigroups, where inverses need not exist.

Finite groups are studied through their subgroups, their actions on sets and their representations by matrices, and the finite simple groups, the building blocks, have been classified. Infinite groups are often studied geometrically, by viewing a group as a space through its Cayley graph, or through presentations by generators and relations. Groups appear throughout mathematics and science, from Galois theory and topology to crystallography and particle physics.

Symmetries of a square

A square has eight symmetries: rotations by 0, 90, 180 and 270 degrees, and four reflections. Performing one after another gives a symmetry again, so they form a group. Order matters: rotating by 90 degrees and then reflecting in the vertical axis gives a different result from reflecting first and then rotating. The four rotations form a subgroup, and 4 divides 8, as Lagrange's theorem requires.

What to notice
  • Starting from just A and B, the number of distinct orientations keeps growing and then stops at 24. No further move can add a new one.
  • The count is exact: the cube's orientation is stored as a rotation matrix, and two orientations are the same only when their matrices are equal.
  • Four A turns, or three B turns, bring the cube back to where it started.

Central objects

Group
A set with an associative operation, an identity element and an inverse for every element.
Subgroup
A subset of a group that is itself a group under the same operation.
Homomorphism
A map between groups that respects the operation.
Normal subgroup
A subgroup stable under conjugation, so that its cosets form a quotient group.
Group action
A rule by which each group element permutes a set, compatibly with the operation.
Simple group
A group with more than one element whose only normal subgroups are itself and the trivial subgroup.
Presentation
A description of a group by generators and the relations they satisfy.

    If HHH is a subgroup of a finite group GGG, then the order of HHH divides the order of GGG.

    Every subgroup of a free group is free.

Open problems and frontiers

  • Amenability of Thompson's group F

    It is not known whether Thompson's group FFF of piecewise linear maps of the interval is amenable.

  • Residual finiteness of hyperbolic groups

    Is every hyperbolic group residually finite, that is, can each nontrivial element be detected in some finite quotient?

  • Finitely presented torsion groups

    Is there an infinite group with finitely many generators and relations in which every element has finite order?
