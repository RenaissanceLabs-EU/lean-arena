41 Approximations and expansions

Approximation theory

How well can a complicated function be imitated by a polynomial of degree ten?

Approximation theory asks how well complicated functions can be replaced by simpler ones such as polynomials, rational functions, trigonometric sums, splines or wavelets. For a given function and a given family, it studies whether a best approximation exists, whether it is unique, how to recognize it, and how fast the error falls as the family grows. Asymptotic expansions, which approximate functions near a limit point, also belong here.

A recurring theme is that the speed of approximation mirrors smoothness: the smoother the function, the faster polynomials converge to it, and conversely. Proofs use explicit operators, moduli of continuity, extremal polynomials and duality from functional analysis. The area supplies the theoretical base for numerical analysis and has close ties to Fourier analysis, interpolation, and more recently to learning theory and compressed representations of data.

Central objects

Best approximation
An element of a given family at the smallest possible distance from the target function.
Modulus of continuity
The largest change of a function over any two points at most a given distance apart.
Chebyshev polynomial
The polynomial TnT_nTn​ with Tn(cos⁡θ)=cos⁡nθT_n(\cos\theta)=\cos n\thetaTn​(cosθ)=cosnθ; it has extremal properties on [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1].
Interpolation
Choosing a simple function that matches the target at prescribed points.
Spline
A function made of polynomial pieces joined with a prescribed degree of smoothness.
Padé approximant
A rational function whose power series agrees with a given series to the highest possible order.
Asymptotic expansion
A series, possibly divergent, whose partial sums approximate a function increasingly well near a limit point.

    Every continuous real function on a closed bounded interval [a,b][a,b][a,b] is a uniform limit of polynomials.
